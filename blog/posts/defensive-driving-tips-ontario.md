Defensive driving is not about driving slowly. It is about spotting trouble early, leaving room to react, and making sure others know what you will do. Ontario's official Driver's Handbook calls it "defensive or strategic driving," and it is the same set of habits examiners look for on the G2 and G road tests.

Here is the handbook's approach as habits you can practise on your next drive.

## What is defensive driving in Ontario?

The MTO Driver's Handbook describes defensive driving as being able to see dangerous situations before they happen and respond quickly and effectively to prevent them. It builds the idea on three pillars:

| Pillar | What it means | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visibility | Seeing and being seen | Scan ahead and to the sides, check mirrors every five seconds or so, use your signals |
| Space | A cushion around your car | Stay well back from the vehicle ahead and leave room on both sides |
| Communication | Others know your plan | Signal early, make eye contact at intersections, use your horn when needed |

The handbook also makes a point many new drivers miss: everyone is responsible for avoiding collisions. Even if someone else does something wrong, you may be found responsible if you could have done something to avoid it. Defensive driving is how you cover for other people's mistakes.

## Tip 1: Look far ahead and keep your eyes moving

The farther ahead you look, the less likely you are to be surprised. Instead of staring at the bumper in front of you, look down the road to see brake lights, turning traffic and pedestrians before they become a problem.

Build a scanning routine:

- **Ahead:** look well down the road, not just at the car in front.
- **To the sides:** watch driveways, parked cars and sidewalks. The handbook warns that people in parked vehicles may pull out or open a door.
- **Behind:** check your mirrors every five seconds or so.
- **Blind spots:** turn your head and look over your shoulder before every lane change or move away from the curb.

## Tip 2: Use the two second rule, and stretch it

Because the greatest risk of a collision is in front of you, the handbook says to stay well back. A safe following distance is **at least two seconds** behind the vehicle ahead.

How to check it:

1. Pick a marker ahead, such as a road sign or pole.
2. When the rear of the vehicle ahead passes it, count "one thousand and one, one thousand and two."
3. If the front of your car reaches the marker before you finish, you are too close.

Two seconds is the minimum, for ideal conditions only. Leave more space in rain or snow, behind motorcycles or large trucks, and when you are carrying a heavy load. Our [winter driving tips for new drivers](/blog/winter-driving-tips-new-drivers/) explain how much extra room slippery roads need.

## Tip 3: Keep a space cushion on all sides

Space is not only about the car ahead. Leave room behind and to both sides so you always have somewhere to go if something goes wrong.

- **Stay out of blind spots.** Do not ride beside other vehicles, especially large commercial trucks, which have big blind spots to the sides and back.
- **Keep others out of yours.** If someone is sitting in your blind spot, adjust your speed slightly so they are visible or ahead of you.
- **Slow down for conditions.** Drive below the posted limit in bad weather, heavy traffic and construction zones.

On the highway, space management matters even more. Our [Highway 401 merging tips](/blog/highway-401-merging-tips/) cover gaps, lane choice and trucks at highway speed.

## Tip 4: Communicate before you act

Other drivers cannot read your mind. The handbook asks you to be predictable: do what other road users expect you to do.

- **Signal well before** you slow down, stop, turn, change lanes or pull away from the curb.
- **Check that the way is clear** after signalling. A signal tells people your plan; it does not give you the right to move.
- **Make eye contact** with pedestrians, cyclists and drivers at intersections so you know they have seen you.

## Tip 5: Expect other drivers to make mistakes

Defensive drivers assume the other person might not stop, might not see them, or might turn without signalling. The handbook tells you to anticipate other drivers' movements and make allowances for every possible error.

That means covering your brake near turning cars and giving cyclists extra room. At intersections, look left, right and left again before you go, even on a green light.

## Tip 6: Stay calm and avoid road rage

Tailgating, speeding, failing to yield and cutting people off all make other drivers angry, and angry drivers do dangerous things. The handbook's advice is simple:

- Do not compete with other drivers or retaliate.
- Do not try to "educate" someone. Leave enforcement to the police.
- If you make a mistake, indicate that you are sorry.

If you ever feel threatened by another driver, stay in your car with the doors locked, call police if you can, and do not drive home if you think you are being followed. Drive to a police station or a busy public place instead.

## Tip 7: Never drive tired or distracted

The handbook says tired drivers can be as impaired as drunk drivers. Warning signs include frequent yawning, trouble keeping your eyes open, drifting out of your lane and not remembering the last few kilometres. If you notice any of them, pull over somewhere safe and rest.

Distraction is just as risky. In Ontario it is illegal to use a hand-held phone to text or dial while driving, including when you are stopped at a red light, except to call 911 in an emergency. Put your phone on silent and out of reach before you start the car. Penalties are steeper for G1 and G2 drivers, so check the current rules on ontario.ca.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the three parts of defensive driving?

Ontario's Driver's Handbook names visibility, space and communication. See and be seen, keep a cushion of space around your car, and make sure other road users know what you are going to do.

### What is the two second rule in Ontario?

Stay at least two seconds behind the vehicle ahead, measured by counting from when it passes a fixed marker until you reach the same marker. It is a minimum for ideal conditions, so leave more room in bad weather or behind trucks and motorcycles.

### How often should I check my mirrors?

The handbook says to check your mirrors every five seconds or so, and to turn your head to check blind spots before changing lanes or pulling out.

### What should I do if another driver is aggressive?

Do not react or gesture back. Give them space, let them pass, and if you feel threatened, stay in your locked car, call police and drive to a busy public place rather than home.
