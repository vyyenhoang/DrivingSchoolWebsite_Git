Nervous About Your Road Test? How to Beat Road Test Anxiety

By Public Star Driving School · November 17, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

Road test anxiety mostly comes from uncertainty, so the best fix is to remove surprises: practise the exact tasks on the test (roadside stop, three-point turn, parking, lane changes) until they feel routine, drive the roads near your DriveTest centre, and take a mock test with an instructor. On test day, arrive early, take a warm-up drive, breathe slowly before you start, and if you make a small mistake, let it go and keep driving safely, because one minor error rarely decides the result.

Almost everyone feels nervous before a road test. You have a stranger with a clipboard in the passenger seat, a licence on the line and a route you cannot see in advance. A little adrenaline is normal and can even keep you alert. The problem is when nerves make you forget your blind spot check or freeze at a busy left turn.

The good news is that road test anxiety is very fixable. Most of it comes from not knowing what will happen, and you can take most of that unknown away with the right preparation. This guide covers what to do in the weeks before your test, on the morning of the test, and in the car itself.

Why do I get so nervous before my driving test?

Test nerves usually come from a few predictable places:

  • Fear of the unknown. You do not know the route, the examiner or which tasks you will be asked to do.
  • Feeling watched. Driving with a family member is different from driving while someone marks every move.
  • High stakes. Rebooking costs time and money, and you may need the licence for school or work.
  • A past bad experience. A failed test or a near miss can stick with you.

Notice that most of these are about uncertainty, not about your actual driving skill. That is why preparation works so well: the more of the test you have already done many times, the less room there is for nerves.

How to prepare in the weeks before your road test

Practise every test task until it is boring

You will not know in advance exactly what the examiner will ask for, so practise all of it. On the G2 road test that means roadside stops and three-point turns, parallel parking, lane changes, left turns at busy intersections and school zones. The goal is for each task to feel like a routine you could do half asleep. Routines hold up under pressure much better than things you are still thinking through.

Learn the roads near your test centre

Every DriveTest centre has its own mix of speed changes, school zones and tricky intersections. Driving those streets ahead of time turns the test route from a mystery into familiar ground. Our Metro East DriveTest tips and Oshawa DriveTest tips cover two centres our students use most.

Take a mock road test

A mock test with an instructor is the closest thing to the real experience. Your instructor gives directions the way an examiner would, stays quiet in between, and tells you afterwards exactly what would have cost you marks. Doing this once or twice takes a lot of the shock out of test day.

Know what actually fails a test

A lot of anxiety comes from imagining that any small slip means instant failure. It does not. Minor mistakes add up on the score sheet, while dangerous actions like rolling a stop sign or an unsafe lane change are what usually end a test. Reading our guide on G2 road test automatic fails helps you focus your worry on the few things that really matter.

Use slow breathing before you start

Before you pull away, take a few slow breaths: in through your nose for about four seconds, hold briefly, then out through your mouth a little longer than you breathed in. Slower breathing helps settle your body, and it gives you a moment to set your mirrors and focus.

Drive in the car you trained in

A car you know removes one more source of stress: you already know where the signals, wipers and hazard lights are, and how the brakes feel. If you do not have a suitable car, our guide to using a rented or driving school car for your road test explains your options.

How to stay calm during the test

  • Talk yourself through it quietly. Some drivers find it helps to think "mirror, signal, blind spot" before every lane change. It keeps your mind on the job.
  • Ask if you did not hear an instruction. Examiners expect this. Asking is far better than guessing.
  • Make your checks obvious. Turn your head for blind spots and scan intersections clearly so the examiner can see you doing it.
  • Drive at a steady, legal speed. Driving very slowly out of nervousness can count against you too.
  • Reset after a mistake. If you miss a check or stop a little far from the line, let it go. Dwelling on it is how one small error turns into three.

What if I still fail because of nerves?

It happens, and it is not the end of the road. Ask the examiner for your score sheet and look at what was marked. Usually it is one or two habits that showed up under pressure, which a couple of focused lessons can fix. Then rebook when you feel ready, not just as soon as possible. Many confident drivers passed on their second try.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal to be nervous for a road test?

Yes, very. Most people feel some nerves. A small amount keeps you alert. The aim is not to feel nothing, but to be prepared enough that nerves do not change how you drive.

Can I tell the examiner I am nervous?

Yes. Saying you are a bit nervous is fine and can help you relax. The examiner will still mark the test the same way, so focus on driving safely and making your checks clear.

Does a warm-up lesson before the test help with nerves?

For many drivers it does. A short drive right before the test settles your hands, reminds you of the car and means the test is not your first time behind the wheel that day.

What should I do if I make a mistake during the test?

Keep driving safely and do not try to fix the past. One minor error rarely fails a test on its own, but losing focus after it can lead to more mistakes.

How many lessons do nervous drivers usually need?

It depends on your experience. Drivers who have practised with family often need 3 to 5 hours with an instructor to polish habits, while brand new drivers usually need more.
